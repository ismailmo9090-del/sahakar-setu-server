# Core Indian Cooperative Law

Dense reference on the constitutional framework and the Multi-State Co-operative Societies Act, 2002, for cooperative governance and legal assistance questions. The Act here means the Multi-State Co-operative Societies Act, 2002 (Act 39 of 2002) as amended, including by the Multi-State Co-operative Societies (Amendment) Act, 2023 (Act 11 of 2023). Section numbers refer to that Act.

## 1. The 97th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2011 and Part IXB (Articles 243ZH–243ZT)

The Constitution (Ninety-seventh Amendment) Act, 2011 (in force from 15 February 2012) made three changes relevant to co-operatives.

First, it amended Article 19(1)(c) so that the right reads: all citizens shall have the right "to form associations or unions or co-operative societies." Citizens thus have a fundamental right to form and join a co-operative society, subject to reasonable restrictions under Article 19(4).

Second, it inserted Article 43B into Part IV (Directive Principles): "The State shall endeavour to promote voluntary formation, autonomous functioning, democratic control and professional management of co-operative societies."

Third, it inserted Part IXB, "The Co-operative Societies" (Articles 243ZH–243ZT), a constitutional template for co-operative regulation:

- Article 243ZH: definitions, including "Registrar" (Central Registrar for multi-State societies, State Registrar otherwise), "multi-State co-operative society", "board" and "office bearer".
- Article 243ZI: the State Legislature may by law provide for the incorporation, regulation and winding up of co-operative societies based on voluntary formation, democratic member-control, member-economic participation and autonomous functioning.
- Article 243ZJ: State law must fix the number and term of board members and office bearers — the board cannot exceed twenty-one directors and the term cannot exceed five years — with co-option of up to two expert persons in banking, management, finance or co-operation, and reservation of one seat for Scheduled Castes or Scheduled Tribes and two seats for women on boards of societies having such members.
- Article 243ZK: elections of board members must be held before expiry of the term, free and fair, with an authority constituted by law to superintend, direct and control elections.
- Article 243ZL: supersession or suspension of a board is constrained — ordinarily not exceeding six months (longer for societies carrying on banking business where the applicable law provides), with fresh elections to be held.
- Article 243ZM: audit of accounts must be provided for by law, ordinarily completed within six months of the close of the financial year, with the auditor's right of access to records.
- Articles 243ZN–243ZQ: general body meetings; a member's right to information (243ZO); annual returns (243ZP); offences and penalties for false information and election malpractice (243ZQ).
- Article 243ZR applies Part IXB to multi-State co-operative societies (reading "Legislature of a State", "State Act", "State Government" as "Parliament", "Central Act", "Central Government"); Article 243ZS extends it to Union territories; Article 243ZT continues inconsistent pre-existing laws until amended or repealed for up to one year.

In Union of India v. Rajendra N. Shah (2021) 11 SCC 633 (20 July 2021), the Supreme Court held that Part IXB, insofar as it governs societies functioning wholly within one State, was void for want of ratification by at least half the State Legislatures under the proviso to Article 368(2), since it curtailed the State's exclusive power under Entry 32, List II. Applying severability, the majority (Nariman J., for himself and Gavai J.; Joseph J. dissenting) held Part IXB remains operative for multi-State co-operative societies, whose competence rests on Entry 44, List I; the amendments to Articles 19(1)(c) and 43B were not disturbed.

## 2. The Multi-State Co-operative Societies Act, 2002 — Overview, Objects and Applicability

The Multi-State Co-operative Societies Act, 2002 (Act 39 of 2002) is the Central law governing co-operative societies whose objects are not confined to one State. It received Presidential assent on 3 July 2002, extends to the whole of India, and was brought into force from 19 August 2002. It repealed and replaced the Multi-unit Co-operative Societies Act, 1942 and the Multi-State Co-operative Societies Act, 1984 (sections 103 and 126).

A "multi-State co-operative society" is a society registered or deemed to be registered under this Act (section 3(p)), and includes a "national co-operative society" (a society specified in the Second Schedule, e.g., IFFCO, NAFED, KRIBHCO, NCUI) and a "federal co-operative" whose membership is open only to societies (sections 23–24). A "co-operative society" simpliciter means a society registered under a State law (section 3(h)); a "co-operative bank" is a multi-State society doing banking business (section 3(f)).

Two registration tests are fundamental (section 5): (a) the main objects must be to serve members' interests in more than one State, and (b) the bye-laws must provide for members' social and economic betterment through self-help and mutual aid in accordance with the seven co-operative principles in the First Schedule (voluntary and open membership; democratic member control; member economic participation; autonomy and independence; education, training and information; co-operation among co-operatives; concern for community). The application must be signed, for an all-individual society, by at least fifty persons from each State concerned; for a society of societies, by authorised representatives of at least five societies not all registered in the same State; or by a mix of at least fifty individuals from each of two or more States plus one co-operative society from each of two or more States (section 6(2)). The Central Registrar (section 4) must dispose of the application within four months (section 7); on registration the society becomes a body corporate with perpetual succession and a common seal (section 9).

The long title states the Act's objectives: to consolidate and amend the law relating to co-operative societies "with objects not confined to one State and serving the interests of members in more than one State", to facilitate the voluntary formation and democratic functioning of co-operatives as people's institutions based on self-help and mutual aid, to promote their economic and social betterment, and to provide functional autonomy. The Act is thus the machinery for the multi-State co-operative sector: uniform registration, democratic member control, timely elections, professional management and central supervision.

By section 2, the Act applies to (a) all societies with objects not confined to one State incorporated before its commencement under the Co-operative Societies Act, 1912, any State law, or the 1942/1984 Central enactments, and (b) all multi-State co-operative societies. A State society may convert into a multi-State society by amending its bye-laws to extend its jurisdiction, registered by the Central Registrar after consulting the State Registrars (section 22). The 2023 Amendment Act tightened norms for thrift-and-credit societies and added prudential requirements, a Co-operative Election Authority, a Co-operative Ombudsman and enhanced penalties.

## 3. Membership under the MSCS Act — Who May Join, Rights, Duties and Disqualifications

Eligibility. Section 25 sets out who may become a member: (a) an individual who is competent to contract under section 11 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 (i.e., of the age of majority and of sound mind, and not disqualified by law); (b) any multi-State or State co-operative society; (c) the Central Government; (d) any State Government; (e) the National Co-operative Development Corporation; (f) any other corporation owned or controlled by the Government; (g) any Government company; and (h) such classes or associations of persons as the Central Registrar permits. No individual may be a member of a national co-operative society or a federal co-operative (section 25(2)); such bodies are federations of societies.

Admission. Membership is by application; the society must dispose of each application within four months and communicate the decision within fifteen days, silence being deemed a refusal (section 25(4)). No member may exercise membership rights unless he has made the payment, or acquired the interest, specified in the bye-laws (section 28). A society may admit "nominal or associate members" under its bye-laws, who may not subscribe shares and have no vote, no right to be directors and no voice in the general body (section 26).

Disqualification. Section 29 makes a person ineligible if: (a) his business conflicts with or competes with the society's business; (b) he fails to use the minimum level of the society's products or services specified in the bye-laws for two consecutive years (as substituted in 2023); (c) he has not attended three consecutive general meetings and the absence was not condoned; or (d) he has defaulted in payment of any amount due to the society under its bye-laws.

Cessation. Membership ends by death, expulsion, or ceasing to fulfil eligibility. A society may expel a member, by a two-thirds majority of members present and voting at a general meeting, for acts detrimental to its proper working, provided the member has a reasonable opportunity to make representations (section 30(1)); an expelled member cannot be re-admitted for three years (section 30(2), as amended in 2023). On death, the society transfers the deceased member's shares/interest to the nominee or legal heir, or pays its value, with consent (section 36). A past member (and the estate of a deceased member) remains liable for the debts existing on the date of cessation or death for two years, and through completion of liquidation if the society is wound up within that window (section 37).

Rights and duties. Every member has one vote in the affairs of the society (section 31), exercised in person — voting by proxy is barred (section 32). Members elect the board at the general meeting, approve the disposal of net profits, the audit and annual reports, the budget and the annual plan, and expel members (sections 38–39). Each member has a duty to promote and protect the interests and objects of the society (section 25(5)), and the society must run co-operative education programmes for members, directors and employees (section 27).

## 4. Board of Directors and Management under the MSCS Act

Every multi-State co-operative society must have a board of directors (section 41), the governing body to which management is entrusted (section 3(b)); directors are elected by members in the general meeting (section 41(2)).

Composition. The board consists of the number of directors fixed by the bye-laws, not exceeding twenty-one (section 41(3)). For societies whose members include individuals, at least one director must be from the Scheduled Castes or Scheduled Tribes and two must be women (as substituted in 2023). The board may co-opt up to two members with experience in banking, management, finance or a related field; co-opted directors may not vote in elections of office bearers or be elected office bearers. Functional directors of national co-operative societies sit on the board but are excluded from counting the twenty-one. A director interested in a contract with the society must not vote on it, and no relative of a sitting director may be recruited as an employee including as Chief Executive (section 41(6)–(7), inserted in 2023).

Office bearers and disqualifications. Ministers of the Union or a State cannot be chairperson/president or vice-chairperson/vice-president (section 44(1)); the chairperson/president can hold office for only two consecutive terms, becoming eligible again after a one-term break (section 44(2)). Section 43 bars from the board persons who are insolvent or directors of insolvent companies, are convicted of an offence involving moral turpitude, hold an office of profit under the society (saving the Chief Executive, notified full-time employees and employee representatives), have been members for less than twelve months, have defaulted on loans or credit from the society, act as legal practitioners for or against the society, are convicted under the Act, are disqualified as members under section 29, have been expelled, or have been absent from three consecutive sittings. A director of a superseded board is disqualified from directorship of another multi-State society or co-operative bank for five years (section 43(1A), inserted in 2023), and a board that fails to hold elections, call the annual general meeting, or prepare and present financial statements is disqualified for five years (section 43(2)).

Powers and functions. The board admits members, appoints and removes the Chief Executive, regulates staff service conditions, places the annual report, financial statements, plans and budgets before the general body, considers audit and compliance reports, acquires or disposes of immovable property, raises funds and sanctions loans (section 49), and must meet at least once every quarter (section 50).

Chief Executive. The board appoints a full-time Chief Executive (section 51) who runs day-to-day management, operates bank accounts, signs documents, maintains books and returns, makes appointments, assists the board in policy and planning, and presents the draft annual report and financial statements within forty-five days of the close of the financial year (section 52). The board constitutes an Executive Committee and other committees, and must have an Audit and Ethics Committee and a committee on prevention of sexual harassment (section 53, as amended in 2023).

## 5. Elections to the Board under the MSCS Act

Democracy is guaranteed by one-member-one-vote: every member, including an employee-member, has one vote in the society's affairs (section 31). An employee-member cannot, however, vote in board elections or in general meetings that frame or amend bye-laws. Voting is in person; proxies are prohibited, though an institutional member (a society, the Central or State Government, etc.) votes through a person it nominates or authorises (sections 31–32).

Election machinery. The 2023 Amendment Act replaced section 45 and created the Co-operative Election Authority: the Central Government establishes, by notification, an Authority comprising a Chairperson, Vice-Chairperson and not more than three Members, appointed on the recommendation of a prescribed Selection Committee (section 45). The Chairperson must have held the post of Additional Secretary to the Government of India or equivalent rank, the Vice-Chairperson Joint Secretary rank; members need the prescribed qualifications. Members serve three years or until age sixty-five, whichever is earlier, and are re-appointable.

Conduct of elections. Elections of board members are conducted in the general meeting by secret ballot, in the prescribed manner (section 45J). The term of elected board members and office bearers is five years from the date of election, office bearers' terms being co-terminus with the board. Casual vacancies can be filled by nomination from the same class of members, but only up to one-third of the elected directors and only when less than half the board's original term remains; vacancies beyond one-third must be filled by election. The Chairperson and Chief Executive must inform the Authority at least six months before the board's term expires, and the society must provide the Authority the infrastructure, personnel, documents and funds needed. Elections must be held before the term expires. The Authority appoints Returning Officers and Assistant Returning Officers (section 45K), appoints observers to supervise polls, and may issue directions — binding on the board, its members, the Chief Executive and staff — for free and fair elections (section 45L). Election expenses are borne by the society. The Chairperson and Vice-Chairperson (President and Vice-President) are elected by the board from amongst its elected members per the Authority's directions, and the Chief Executive issues the certificate of election after the resolution is concluded (section 49(2)(aa)).

Accountability and timelines. If the board fails to call the annual general meeting in which elections are to be held, the Central Registrar may convene it within ninety days (section 39(2)). A board that fails to provide the Authority required assistance, hold elections, call the AGM, or get the accounts audited within six months of the close of the financial year can have its members disqualified from re-election for five years (section 43(2)). Electoral malpractice is punishable under section 104(4), and a person convicted of gratification offences is debarred from contesting co-operative elections for three years (2023 amendment).

## 6. Audit of Multi-State Co-operative Societies (Sections 70–77)

Every multi-State co-operative society must have its accounts audited at least once every year (section 70(1)). The auditor is appointed at each annual general meeting and holds office until the conclusion of the next; the society must notify the auditor within seven days. Auditors are chosen from a panel approved by the Central Registrar or prepared by the society. The first auditor is appointed by the board within one month of registration; if no auditor is appointed or the vacancy is not filled, the Central Registrar fills it. A retiring auditor must be re-appointed unless disqualified, unwilling, replaced by resolution, or the proposed replacement cannot act (sections 70–71).

Qualifications. The auditor must be a chartered accountant (section 72). A body corporate, an officer or employee of the society, a person employed by such officer/employee, a person indebted to the society, or a person guaranteeing a third person's debt to the society beyond one thousand rupees, is disqualified; disqualification after appointment vacates office.

Powers and duties. The auditor has a right of access at all times to the books, accounts and vouchers wherever kept, and may require any information and explanations from officers and employees (section 73). The auditor must inquire whether loans and advances are properly secured and on terms not prejudicial to the society or members, whether transactions represented by book entries alone are prejudicial, whether personal expenses have been charged to revenue, and whether shares said to be allotted for cash have actually received the cash. The auditor reports to the members on the balance-sheet and profit and loss account, and the report must state whether the accounts present a true and fair view and whether proper books have been kept, branch returns are adequate, and the balance-sheet agrees with the books; reasons must be given for qualified or negative answers. Only the appointed auditor may sign the audit report (section 74). The report is read before the general meeting and is open to inspection by members (section 75); the auditor is entitled to receive meeting notices, attend general meetings and be heard (section 76). Remuneration is fixed by the board or Central Registrar where they appoint, otherwise by the general meeting.

Regularity and downstream consequences. The annual general meeting must be called not later than six months after the close of the financial year and must consider the audited accounts, audit report, annual report, audit compliance report and disposal of net profits (section 39(1)). The Central Government may direct a special audit in the public interest (section 77). The Central Registrar may hold inquiries (section 78) and cause inspection of the society or of the books of indebted societies (sections 79–80); audit, special audit, inquiry or inspection may furnish the ground for winding up (section 86(1)). Failure to complete the audit within six months exposes the board to a five-year election disqualification (section 43(2)(f), inserted in 2023), mirroring Part IXB (Article 243ZM).

## 7. Financial Structure — Funds, Reserves and Distribution of Net Profits (ss. 62–67)

Non-profit discipline. The Act preserves the co-operative rule that a society is not a dividend-maximising company: no part of the funds, other than net profits, may be divided among members by way of bonus, dividend or otherwise (section 62(1)). "Net profits" are gross profits less overdue-interest accruals, establishment charges, interest on loans and deposits, audit fees, working expenses (repairs, rent, taxes, depreciation), employee bonus and the bonus equalisation fund, income-tax provision and approved donations, development rebate, and provisions for the development, bad debt, price-fluctuation, dividend-equalisation, share-capital redemption, investment-fluctuation and retirement-benefit funds, after writing off bad debts and losses not adjusted against a fund created out of profits (section 62(2)). Capital-less societies treat surplus of income over expenditure under their bye-laws, not as net profits.

Compulsory appropriations. Out of net profits, every society must (section 63(1)): (a) transfer not less than twenty-five per cent to a reserve fund; (b) credit annually one per cent of net profit to a co-operative education fund maintained by the Central Government, which funds education and training through the National Co-operative Union of India and other agencies (as substituted in 2023); and (c) transfer not less than ten per cent to a reserve fund for unforeseen losses. It cannot declare dividends out of capital.

Disposal of the balance. The balance of net profits may be applied, subject to prescribed conditions (section 63(2)): dividend to members on paid-up share capital at a rate not exceeding the prescribed limit; contribution to special funds, including education funds, specified in the bye-laws; donation of up to five per cent of net profits for development of the co-operative movement or a charitable purpose; and ex gratia payments to employees as provided in the bye-laws. There is no statutory "patronage refund"; the member's commercial return is the capped dividend plus the benefit of services transacted with the society.

Share capital and holding limits. The maximum shareholding of any single member (other than the Government bodies, co-operative societies and institutions in section 25(1)(c)–(g)) is prescribed by rules or bye-laws and may not exceed one-fifth of total share capital (section 33). Transfers of shares are subject to the same limits (section 34); Government-held shares are redeemable at face value under the bye-laws or agreement, and after 2023 Government shares under clauses (c)–(d) may not be redeemed without those authorities' prior approval (section 35).

Investment and borrowing. Funds must be placed in co-operative banks, in securities under section 20 of the Indian Trusts Act, 1882, in shares/securities of other co-operative societies, in subsidiary institutions, in any bank, or in modes fixed by bye-laws (section 64). Deposits and loans during any financial year cannot exceed ten times subscribed share capital plus accumulated reserves, net of losses (section 67(1)); non-convertible debentures can raise up to twenty-five per cent of paid-up share capital (section 67(3)). Loans on the security of shares, or to non-members, are barred except for co-operative banks (section 66). The 2023 amendments established the Co-operative Rehabilitation, Reconstruction and Development Fund for sick societies (sections 63A–63C).

## 8. Amalgamation, Division, Liquidation and Winding Up

Structural changes. A society may, by a two-thirds-majority general-body resolution, transfer its assets and liabilities, in whole or in part, to another multi-State or State society, divide itself into two or more societies, or amalgamate with other multi-State societies into a new multi-State society (section 17(1)–(2)). Written notice must go to all members and creditors, each of whom has one month to withdraw shares, deposits or loans; silence is deemed assent, and the resolution takes effect only after all members and creditors have assented, with arrangements made to satisfy those who exercise the option (sections 17(4)–(6)). The Central Registrar then registers the new society or societies unless it refuses for recorded reasons, and the resolution operates as a sufficient conveyance vesting assets and liabilities (sections 17(7)–(9)). For a co-operative bank under a moratorium, the Central Registrar may, with the Reserve Bank's written approval, prepare a scheme of amalgamation or reorganisation (section 18). A State society may similarly convert into a multi-State society by a registered bye-law amendment (section 22). The 2023 amendment requires the written no-objection of institutional lenders with outstanding loans before voluntary winding up.

Winding up. The Central Registrar may direct winding up after an audit (section 70), special audit (section 77), inquiry (section 78) or inspection (sections 79, 108), if of the opinion the society ought to be wound up, after allowing the society to make representations (section 86(1)); or of his own motion where membership has fallen below the section 6(2) minimum (with six months' grace to restore the numbers), where the society has not commenced working within six months of registration or has ceased to function in accordance with co-operative principles, or, after 2023, where registration was obtained by misrepresentation, false information or fraud (section 86(2)). A society may resolve to wind itself up by a two-thirds majority of members present and voting, in which case the Central Registrar shall order it (section 86(6)). Multi-State co-operative banks cannot be wound up without the Reserve Bank's sanction and the Banking Regulation Act, 1949 applies to their winding up (sections 86(5), 87); the Deposit Insurance and Credit Guarantee Corporation is reimbursed where it has paid depositors (section 88).

Liquidation. On the winding-up order the Central Registrar appoints a liquidator (section 89), in whom all assets vest; he takes custody of the property, realises it by sale, sues and defends, assesses contributions (including debts and liquidation costs) payable by members, past members, deceased members' estates and former officers, investigates and pays claims in order of priority, makes compromises, carries on the business so far as beneficial, and disposes of surplus assets — per the bye-laws' designated purpose or, if none, to the members with the previous sanction of the Central Government (sections 90–91). Contributions assessed by the liquidator rank next to sums due to the Central or State Government or a local authority (section 92). A winding-up order is appealable (section 99). On the liquidator's report, the Central Registrar cancels the registration and the society stands dissolved (section 93).

## 9. Offences and Penalties under the MSCS Act

The Act's penal provisions are consolidated in sections 104–105 (as substantially strengthened by the 2023 Amendment Act).

False returns and information. A society, officer or member that wilfully makes a false return or furnishes false information, or fails to file a return, or any person who, without reasonable excuse, disobeys a summons, requisition or lawful written order under the Act, is punishable with a fine of not less than five thousand rupees, extendable to one lakh rupees (section 104(1); raised in 2023 from two thousand and ten thousand rupees).

Deduction defaults. An employer who, without sufficient cause, fails to pay over to the society amounts deducted from employees' salary under section 60 within fourteen days of the deduction is liable to a fine of up to ten thousand rupees (section 104(2)).

Failure to hand over records. An officer or custodian who wilfully fails to hand over books, accounts, documents, records, cash, securities or other property to the person entitled under sections 54, 70, 78, 79 or 89 is liable to a fine of up to five thousand rupees plus up to ten thousand rupees for each continuing day after the first conviction (section 104(3)).

Electoral offences. Whoever, before, during or after an election of delegates or board members, fraudulently defaces or destroys nomination papers, ballot papers or official marks, converts or destroys election lists and notices, supplies or possesses ballot papers without authority, stuffs or interferes with ballot boxes, offers gratification to induce a person to stand, withdraw or vote, or aids such acts, is punishable with imprisonment of up to six months, or fine, or both (section 104(4)). A person who gives or receives such gratification is additionally debarred from contesting co-operative elections for three years (2023 amendment).

Document destruction, unlawful investment and losses (inserted 2023). Where a society destroys, mutilates, falsifies, conceals or removes any document relating to its property or affairs, makes false entries, invests in contravention of section 64, or causes unlawful loss to the society or to depositors, the board or responsible officers are punishable with imprisonment of one month to one year, or a fine of five thousand to one lakh rupees, or both (section 104(5)). Directors or officers who receive unlawful gains from society transactions or use society assets for personal gain face the same punishment and must disgorge the proceeds (section 104(6)).

Falsification and misappropriation are also addressed at the civil level: the Central Registrar can seize records (section 54), inspect books (section 108), hold inquiries (section 78) and use the irregularities as grounds for winding up. Theft, criminal breach of trust and cheating remain punishable under the Indian Penal Code, the Act being in addition to and not in derogation of any other law (section 105A, inserted 2023).

Prosecution. No court inferior to a Metropolitan Magistrate or Judicial Magistrate of the First Class may try offences under the Act, and no prosecution under section 104 lies except on a written complaint by a member or the Central Registrar (section 105).

## 10. State Co-operative Society vs Multi-State Co-operative Society

The central jurisdictional divide in Indian co-operative law is between a society registered under a State Co-operative Societies Act and one registered under the MSCS Act, 2002.

Legislative competence. Co-operative societies other than multi-State societies fall within Entry 32, List II (State List), and Part IXB does not bind purely State societies after Union of India v. Rajendra N. Shah (2021) 11 SCC 633. Multi-State co-operative societies, having objects not confined to one State, fall within Entry 44, List I (Union List), are governed by the MSCS Act, 2002, and Part IXB applies through Article 243ZR.

Registration authority. A State society is registered by the Registrar of Co-operative Societies of its State, under that State's Act, with jurisdiction confined to that State. A multi-State society is registered by the Central Registrar, can admit members and do business across States, and is a body corporate under Central law.

Membership geography. A State society's members and area of operation lie within one State; a multi-State society's main objects must serve members in more than one State, and an all-individual society needs at least fifty members from each of two or more States (section 6(2)).

Supervision and audit. State societies are audited, inspected and supervised by the State Registrar; multi-State societies are audited annually by chartered accountants under sections 70–77 and are open to inquiry, inspection and special audit by the Central Registrar and Central Government. Government nominees on the board follow shareholding thresholds (section 48), as in State Acts.

Dispute resolution. Disputes touching the constitution or business of a multi-State society, other than those before authorities under the Act, can be referred to arbitration before the Central Registrar (section 84); his decisions and orders are executed like civil-court decrees (section 94). State societies use their State Acts' arbitration and recovery mechanisms.

Governance timeline. Both systems impose five-year board terms, one-member-one-vote, timely elections and annual audit, but for multi-State societies these are reinforced by the central scheme — the Co-operative Election Authority (section 45), the five-year term and the six-month audit window — while State societies follow their State Act, which post-2021 need not replicate Part IXB and varies.

Conversion. A State society may convert into a multi-State society by amending its bye-laws to extend its jurisdiction, registered by the Central Registrar after consulting the State Registrars (section 22). On registration it becomes a multi-State society from that date, and the State Registrar must order that it has ceased to be a society under State law. The converse — a multi-State society confining itself to one State — requires it to wind up or reorganise into a State society.

Practical consequence. For legal assistance, the answer to any question about a society's membership, elections, board, audit, disputes or winding up turns first on which law registered it: State law and the State Registrar before the courts of that State, or the MSCS Act 2002 and the Central Registrar under Union jurisdiction. The chatbot must establish registration first, since it determines the entire applicable framework.
